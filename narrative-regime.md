# Narrative regime

## Status of the concept

The “narrative regime” is an analytical concept introduced by Lega et al. to describe an evidentiary configuration in which justification relies primarily on the coherence and integration of heterogeneous sources of evidence when independent experimental replication is limited or absent [1].

It is not a formal regulatory category. The term “narrative” should not be interpreted as meaning rhetorical, anecdotal, or subjective. It refers to an architecture of inference in which several evidentiary components are connected into an integrated justificatory argument.

## Definition

A narrative regime is an evidentiary configuration in which a target claim is justified through the coherence of multiple, partly heterogeneous components of evidence rather than primarily through independent replication of the same experimental result.

Relevant components may include randomized or non-randomized clinical data, surrogate endpoints, pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic information, exposure–response relationships, mechanistic knowledge, external controls, natural-history data, real-world evidence, extrapolation across populations or formulations, and prior evidence obtained in related settings.

The evidentiary force of the argument therefore depends not only on the validity of each individual component, but also on the validity of the inferential links connecting them.

## Three evidentiary configurations

Lega et al. distinguish three partly coexisting configurations [1].

| Evidentiary configuration | Principal basis of justification | Simplified inferential structure |
|---|---|---|
| Two-trial paradigm | Independent experimental replication | Trial 1 → claim; Trial 2 → same claim |
| One-trial paradigm | One adequate pivotal trial supported by confirmatory evidence | Pivotal trial + confirmatory evidence → claim |
| Narrative regime | Coherence across heterogeneous evidence when independent replication is limited or absent | Evidence A + Evidence B + Evidence C + inferential links → integrated claim |

These configurations should not be interpreted as mutually exclusive regulatory categories. They describe different dominant architectures of evidentiary justification.

## From replication to integration

In a replication-based architecture, the central question is whether an empirical finding can be reproduced independently.

In a narrative regime, the central question becomes:

> Do several sources of evidence, linked by defensible assumptions, jointly support the target claim?

The inferential structure can be represented as:

```text
Evidence components
        ↓
Inferential links and assumptions
        ↓
Assessment of coherence and contradictions
        ↓
Integrated evidentiary claim
```

The shift is therefore not simply from “more evidence” to “less evidence”. It is a shift in how evidence acquires justificatory force.

Under replication, credibility is strengthened because independent observations reproduce the target finding. Under narrative integration, credibility is strengthened because different sources of information are judged to form a coherent inferential structure. These mechanisms are epistemically distinct.

## Structure of a narrative inference

A useful decomposition is:

```text
Evidence node 1 ──┐
Evidence node 2 ──┼──→ inferential links ──→ target claim
Evidence node 3 ──┘
```

Each arrow contains an assumption. The assumptions may concern transportability, causal relevance, measurement validity, dose–exposure relationships, exposure–response relationships, surrogate validity, comparability of populations, or the absence of clinically relevant effect modification.

Consequently, appraisal should distinguish three levels:

1. the internal validity of each evidence node;
2. the validity of the inferential links connecting nodes;
3. the robustness of the integrated claim to discordant or contradictory evidence.

## Conditions for a strong narrative inference

A narrative inference is more defensible when three properties are simultaneously present.

First, the individual evidence components have adequate internal validity.

Second, the inferential links connecting those components are explicit and empirically or mechanistically justified.

Third, potentially contradictory evidence is actively sought and incorporated rather than excluded from the narrative.

A conceptual representation is:

```text
Strength of narrative inference
≈ validity of components
× validity of inferential links
× robustness to contradictory evidence
```

This expression is not a quantitative model. It emphasizes that a major failure in one part of the inferential architecture may not be compensated merely by accumulating additional concordant observations.

## Worked example: IV-to-SC bridging

The development of a subcutaneous (SC) formulation of a biologic previously established through intravenous (IV) administration provides a useful example of a narrative evidentiary architecture [2].

The target claim is not necessarily established by repeating the complete IV clinical development programme with the SC formulation. Instead, previously established IV evidence is connected to new pharmacokinetic, exposure–response, comparative clinical, safety, and immunogenicity evidence through explicit bridging assumptions.

A simplified structure is:

```text
Established IV clinical efficacy and safety
                    ↓
Characterisation of SC pharmacokinetics
                    ↓
Comparison of relevant exposure metrics
                    ↓
Exposure–response knowledge
                    ↓
Comparative clinical data
                    ↓
Safety and immunogenicity
                    ↓
Integrated IV → SC bridging claim
```

No single component necessarily establishes the complete claim. Pharmacokinetic comparability alone does not establish clinical equivalence. Limited comparative clinical data do not necessarily reproduce the full IV evidence base. Exposure–response modelling depends on assumptions concerning which exposure metrics are clinically relevant. Safety and immunogenicity may differ after changes in route, formulation, dosing interval, absorption pattern, peak concentration, or local exposure.

The conclusion therefore emerges from the integration of these components rather than from independent replication of the original clinical result.

## Narrative integration is not equivalent to weak evidence

A narrative regime should not be equated with poor-quality evidence.

Some bridging programmes may contain randomized comparisons, high-quality pharmacokinetic studies, validated exposure–response models, extensive prior clinical evidence, and substantial regulatory scrutiny. The distinction concerns the structure of inference rather than simply the methodological quality of individual studies.

Conversely, a collection of individually strong studies does not necessarily produce a strong integrated inference if the links between them are poorly justified.

## Characteristic failure modes

### Hidden assumptions

The bridge between two pieces of evidence may depend on assumptions that are not explicitly stated or empirically tested.

### Circularity

Apparently independent components may derive from the same data, model, endpoint, or underlying assumption and therefore provide less independent support than they appear to.

### Selective coherence

Supportive observations may be integrated while discordant findings are treated as exceptions, creating apparent coherence through selective evidence inclusion.

### Model dependence

A conclusion may depend strongly on pharmacometric, causal, extrapolative, or statistical models whose structural assumptions are difficult to validate empirically.

### Surrogate substitution

Evidence that a treatment affects a biomarker, pharmacokinetic measure, or surrogate endpoint may progressively substitute for evidence concerning the clinical outcome that ultimately motivates the claim.

### Extrapolation beyond the validated bridge

A bridge established for one dose, population, indication, endpoint, or exposure range may be extended beyond the conditions under which its assumptions were evaluated.

### Institutional stabilisation

Once an inferential strategy becomes accepted in regulatory practice, precedent may contribute to the plausibility of similar subsequent arguments. Regulatory precedent can therefore become part of the evidentiary context without constituting independent empirical replication.

## Auditing a narrative evidentiary claim

A narrative claim can be assessed by reconstructing its inferential architecture.

The following questions are particularly useful:

1. What exactly is the target claim?
2. Which empirical observations directly address that claim?
3. Which observations support it only indirectly?
4. What assumptions connect the indirect evidence to the target claim?
5. Which components constitute genuinely independent evidence?
6. Which components reuse the same data or assumptions?
7. What evidence could potentially contradict the proposed narrative?
8. Were such contradictions actively sought?
9. Which inferential link is most vulnerable to failure?
10. How far does the conclusion extend beyond the populations, doses, outcomes, formulations, or settings directly studied?
11. What observation would lead to rejection or substantial revision of the narrative?

The last question is especially important. An evidentiary account that can accommodate almost any possible observation through additional explanation has weak empirical refutability, even if the resulting narrative appears coherent.

## Relationship with totality of evidence

The narrative regime overlaps with, but is not synonymous with, the regulatory idea of a “totality of evidence”.

“Totality of evidence” generally refers to considering multiple relevant sources of information when reaching a conclusion. “Narrative regime” is intended primarily as an analytical description of the inferential architecture created when heterogeneous evidence replaces or supplements independent experimental replication.

The concept therefore directs attention not only to which evidence is included, but to how the components are connected and how the resulting claim is justified.

## Relationship with Bayesian reasoning

Narrative integration should also be distinguished from formal Bayesian inference.

Both approaches allow prior knowledge and new evidence to contribute jointly to inference. In a formal Bayesian model, however, the relationship between prior information, likelihood, and posterior inference is explicitly specified mathematically. In a narrative regime, many evidence components and inferential links may remain qualitative or only partly formalised.

Narrative coherence therefore cannot automatically be interpreted as a Bayesian posterior probability.

## Relationship with mechanistic reasoning

Mechanistic evidence can contribute to a narrative regime but is not sufficient to define it. Mechanistic knowledge can provide a bridge between observations and increase the plausibility of extrapolation. The narrative regime concerns the broader architecture through which mechanistic, experimental, observational, pharmacological, and contextual evidence are combined.

## Relationship with extrapolation

Extrapolation is one mechanism that can operate within a narrative regime. Examples include extrapolation from adults to children, from one indication to another, from IV to SC administration, from a surrogate endpoint to a clinical outcome, from trial populations to populations not directly studied, or from historical/external controls to a new single-arm study.

The narrative regime is broader because it concerns the evidentiary structure used to justify the resulting inference rather than extrapolation itself.

## Scope and limits

The concept was proposed in the context of drug regulation and health technology assessment [1]. Potential applications include regulatory extrapolation, model-informed drug development, rare-disease evaluation, external-control studies, real-world evidence, surrogate endpoint assessment, and evidence transport across populations or formulations.

These extensions should currently be treated as applications of the analytical framework rather than as independently validated components of a formal taxonomy.

The concept does not imply that regulatory flexibility is intrinsically inappropriate, that two randomized trials are always necessary, that mechanistic or observational evidence lacks evidentiary value, or that narrative inference is necessarily unreliable.

Its purpose is diagnostic rather than prescriptive: to make the architecture of evidentiary reasoning explicit and auditable.

## Key message

The defining question of a replication-based evidentiary regime is:

> Has the finding been independently reproduced?

The defining question of a narrative regime is:

> Do multiple sources of evidence, connected by defensible assumptions, form a sufficiently coherent and contradiction-resistant basis for the claim?

The distinction matters because these two questions do not provide the same protection against false inference.
