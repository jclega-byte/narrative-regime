# Worked example: IV-to-SC bridging

## Why IV-to-SC bridging is informative

The development of a subcutaneous (SC) formulation of a biologic previously established through intravenous (IV) administration provides a concrete example of a narrative evidentiary architecture.

The target claim is not simply that the SC formulation produces the same pharmacokinetic profile as the IV formulation. Nor is the complete IV clinical development programme necessarily repeated. Instead, the inference that therapeutic properties can be transferred from IV to SC administration is constructed across several linked evidence domains [2].

## Generic inferential architecture

```text
Established IV efficacy and safety
              ↓
SC pharmacokinetic characterisation
              ↓
Selection of relevant exposure metric(s)
              ↓
Exposure–response relationship
              ↓
Comparative clinical evidence
              ↓
Safety and immunogenicity
              ↓
IV → SC bridging claim
```

The links are not automatic. They require assumptions such as:

- the IV efficacy evidence is sufficiently established;
- the selected exposure metric is relevant to efficacy and safety;
- the SC regimen covers the clinically relevant exposure range;
- altered absorption kinetics, peak exposure, trough exposure, and dosing frequency do not introduce clinically important effects not captured by the bridge;
- immunogenicity and local tolerability remain acceptable;
- evidence generated in the studied population can support the intended target population and indications.

## CT-P13 infliximab as an illustration

Schreiber et al. compared subcutaneous and intravenous CT-P13 in inflammatory bowel disease [3]. After IV induction, patients were randomized to SC or IV maintenance therapy. The primary endpoint was pharmacokinetic: non-inferiority of observed predose concentration at week 22.

The trial met this criterion. Importantly, the SC regimen did not simply reproduce the IV concentration profile: the reported geometric least-squares mean ratio for trough concentration was substantially above 100%, while clinical remission, safety, and immunogenicity outcomes were broadly comparable between groups [3].

This makes the example particularly useful conceptually. The bridging claim cannot be reduced to:

```text
SC exposure = IV exposure
```

A more accurate representation is:

```text
Established IV efficacy
+
SC exposure judged sufficient relative to the relevant exposure range
+
credible exposure–response knowledge
+
comparative clinical evidence
+
acceptable safety and immunogenicity
+
absence of a clinically important contradiction introduced by the route change

→ integrated IV-to-SC bridging claim
```

## What is being inferred?

The bridge transfers part of the evidentiary force of the established IV programme to the SC formulation. The inference is conditional rather than absolute.

It therefore matters to specify the target claim. Possible claims are not equivalent:

- pharmacokinetic non-inferiority;
- preservation of clinical efficacy;
- preservation of safety;
- acceptability of immunogenicity;
- interchangeability or switching;
- extrapolation across indications;
- preservation of the overall benefit–risk balance.

Each claim may require a different evidentiary path.

## Why this is a narrative regime

No single empirical result establishes the complete IV-to-SC claim.

The evidentiary conclusion emerges from the coherence of pharmacokinetic, exposure–response, clinical, safety, and immunogenicity evidence. The key object of appraisal is therefore not only the validity of the component studies but also the validity of the links connecting them.

This is the defining feature of the narrative regime: the target conclusion depends on an integrated inferential chain rather than on independent replication of the same clinical effect.

## How to audit the bridge

A practical audit can reconstruct the bridge as a directed chain of claims and assumptions.

| Step | Question | Potential failure |
|---|---|---|
| Established IV evidence | What therapeutic property is considered established? | Overstatement of the starting evidence base |
| PK metric | Which exposure metric is used for bridging? | Metric not causally or clinically relevant |
| Exposure–response | Is the relation characterised across the relevant range? | Extrapolation outside observed range |
| Clinical comparison | What is directly compared? | Trial too small or endpoint too indirect for the intended claim |
| Safety | Are altered peak/trough patterns clinically relevant? | Safety differences masked by limited exposure |
| Immunogenicity | Does route change modify immunogenicity? | Inadequate follow-up or insensitive assessment |
| Transport/extrapolation | To which populations or indications is the bridge extended? | Unsupported effect modification assumptions |

## Interpretation

IV-to-SC bridging illustrates why “narrative” should not be equated with “weak”. The component evidence can be methodologically strong. The distinctive feature lies in the inferential architecture: different evidence types perform different roles, and the final claim depends on the credibility of their connections.

The principal risk is therefore not only bias within individual studies. It is failure of an inferential link that may remain implicit unless the whole argument is reconstructed.
